01

Why the format matters

Marshall recommends its own resume format for on-campus recruiting. Firms that recruit at USC see it all season, so a resume in the house format reads as "Trojan, and prepared" before anyone reads a word.

It signals where you're from

The layout is recognizable to recruiters who hire at USC. It tells them you used Marshall's resources.

It's fast to scan

Resumes often get a few seconds on the first pass. A layout the reader already knows lets them find your school, GPA, and roles at a glance.

It removes easy mistakes

Fonts, dates, and spacing are already decided, so your time goes into the part that wins interviews: the bullets.

Marshall describes a resume as a personal advertisement. Its job is to get you the interview, not to list everything you've done.

02

The format in eight rules

These come straight from the Marshall resume guide. Everything else on this page builds on them.

  • 1One page, with consistent margins.
  • 2Times New Roman, 10 to 12 point, black text.
  • 3Education goes at the top.
  • 4Education, Experience, and Skills sections are required.
  • 5Reverse chronological order: most recent first.
  • 6Every experience lists its location and dates.
  • 7Consistent abbreviations, and no unexplained acronyms.
  • 8Consistent alignment, spacing, and bullet points throughout.

Quick check: a friend's resume runs onto a second page because of a long Activities list. What's the Marshall fix?

One page and 10 to 12 point are both rules, and Education stays on top. Marshall's advice is to skip the laundry list and keep what's relevant to the job you're applying for.

04

Same job, different resume

You don't need a new job for every application. You need a new version of the jobs you already have.

The facts stay fixed

Employer, title, and dates never change between versions. Only the bullets do.

Lead with what they want

Read the job description, then put the true details that match it first, in its words.

Keep a master resume

Write every bullet you could ever use in one long file. Each application is a cut of it.

Open an experience, then flip between the four versions. Highlighted words are the ones that changed to fit each field. The first four come straight from Evelyn's own resume, the person who built this site; the rest are samples.

Which version would you send?

Read each job posting, then pick the version of your resume that fits it.

Summer Analyst, Strategy: "Work on client teams to structure business problems, interview stakeholders, analyze data, and turn findings into clear recommendations."

Structure, interviews, analysis, and recommendations: the consulting versions lead with exactly that language.

Brand Marketing Intern: "Help plan product launches, build social content calendars, and track campaign results to grow our audience."

Launches, social content, and audience growth. Send the version that leads with customers and campaigns.

Staff Accountant Intern: "Support month-end close, prepare account reconciliations, and help track spending against budget."

Reconciliations and budgets. The accounting versions put those details first, even for a coffee job or a club role.

Operations Rotational Program: "Coordinate projects across teams, improve processes, and keep work on schedule."

Coordinating, process, and schedules. The business admin versions show you can run things.

06

Section by section

What goes in each section, and the mistakes that cost points.

Header Required

  • Phone, an email you check, and your LinkedIn URL.
  • Use the same header on your resume, cover letter, and references.
  • Left, center, or right alignment is your choice.
  • Skip your mailing address.

Education Required

  • School, location, degree, graduation month and year.
  • Cumulative USC GPA only. Under 3.0, you may leave it off.
  • Your declared emphasis, honors, and relevant coursework.
  • Study abroad goes here.

Experience Required

  • Internships, summer jobs, part-time work, and work-study all count.
  • Employer, location, title, and dates for every role.
  • Bullets about what you achieved, matched to the job description.
  • Skip the laundry list of every task.

Leadership & Involvement Optional

  • Clubs, teams, and programs where you led or contributed.
  • LInC and GLP go here, not in Education.
  • Several roles in one group stack under the group's name.
  • Don't describe the club. Describe what you did in it.

Skills & Interests Required

  • Technical skills the job asks for, with specifics: Excel (vlookups, pivot tables).
  • A level for every language and tool: fluent, conversational, advanced, basic.
  • Interests are optional. Keep them professional and specific.

Academic Projects Optional

  • Use it when you need more content, or when a class project fits the job better than your work history.
  • Include the course, project name, and date.
  • Use the terms the job description uses.
07

Write bullets that show results

A list of duties tells the reader what your job was. A strong bullet tells them what you achieved and why it mattered.

1 · Action, Context, Result

Marshall's main technique. Start with a strong verb, add who, what, or where, and finish with the outcome. Put a number on it when you can.

BeforeResponsible for social media for a student club
AfterGrew the Trojan Consulting Group's Instagram from 400 to 1,900 followers in one semester, doubling info session sign-ups
ActionContextResult

2 · Pull out transferable skills

A job that isn't business related still shows communication, teamwork, leadership, accuracy, or initiative. Name the skill and show it.

BeforeWorked as a lifeguard
AfterSupervised safety for up to 200 swimmers a shift and trained six new guards on emergency procedures

3 · Give awards context

An award name means nothing to a stranger. Say what it was for and how selective it was.

BeforeRecipient of the Excellence Award
AfterSelected for a firm-wide award given to the top 5% of summer interns for client service

Fix these bullets

Rewrite each one out loud or on paper, then compare.

1. "Helped with event planning for my sorority."

"Planned a formal for 180 members, negotiating venue and catering contracts that came in 12% under budget."

"Helped" hides your role. Name the event, its size, and a result.

2. "Responsible for answering customer emails."

"Resolved 40+ customer emails a day, cutting average response time from two days to six hours."

"Responsible for" is a duty. Start with the action and add the scale and the result.

3. "Was a member of the investing club and went to meetings."

"Pitched a long position on a regional bank to the club's 30-member investment committee, which added it to the portfolio."

Attending isn't an accomplishment. Pick one thing you produced and what came of it.

4. "Did research on competitors for my internship."

"Benchmarked pricing for eight competitors and identified a gap the marketing team used to reposition its entry-level plan."

Say how much research, on what, and what the team did with it.
